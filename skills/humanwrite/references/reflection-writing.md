# Authentic reflective writing

Use for personal reflections, learning journals, and reflective assignments. Preserve the user's actual experience, judgment, uncertainty, and voice. Do not invent meetings, actions, dialogue, emotions, failures, research experience, or growth.

## Obtain enough real material

If the user supplies a meaningful episode and reflection direction, proceed. If direction is unclear, follow the 2–3 direction selection rule in SKILL.md. If factual experience is missing, ask for a brief account of the relevant event, what they thought or did, and what happened. Do not force a second direction-selection step when intent is already clear. Offer a labeled outline with placeholders if the user prefers, rather than a fictional first-person account.

## Develop reflection

Use the following questions to find substance, not as a compulsory sequence of identical paragraphs:
- What specific situation or decision mattered?
- What did the user think, do, say, or choose not to say at the time?
- What was the result, and what evidence connects it to their action?
- Which assumption, limitation, or alternative interpretation can they now identify?
- What would they change in a similar situation, and why?

Replace “I learned the importance of teamwork” with analysis of the supplied episode. Preserve real hesitation, disagreement, discomfort, or self-criticism when the user supplies it. Do not manufacture emotional disclosure, force a negative confession, or make every episode end in a neat success story. Distinguish a hindsight interpretation from what the user knew at the time.

## Apply theory to the experience

Start with the actual event and problem. Use a relevant theory to explain a specific mechanism, then connect that explanation to a possible change. When a required framework such as Gibbs is supplied, respect its required structure without reducing the reflection to generic stage summaries. Verify a theory's meaning before making a specific scholarly claim; do not label an episode groupthink merely because people agreed.

## End with a grounded action

Develop a feasible action tied to the identified problem, with a relevant situation, concrete behavior, and way to review progress. For example, proposing to list concerns before the next meeting may follow from a supplied account of staying silent. Present a new action as a proposal or future intention, never as something the user already did or agreed to do. Avoid vague promises to “be more confident” without explaining what changes.

## Review

Check experience → interpretation → theory → action for consistency and evidential support. Remove claims of growth unsupported by the episode. If essential personal details are unknown, keep them explicitly unresolved. Writing quality must not depend on fabricating authenticity.
